How to write a home inspection report

A good home inspection report is clear, complete and easy for the client to act on. This guide covers what to check, how to document each finding, how to rate severity and how to send a professional PDF. A free checklist is included below.

Jump to the steps

Step 1: Set up the inspection

Before you walk the property, record the basics: property address, date and time, client name and contact, and who is attending. Note the weather and whether the utilities are on. These details make the report credible and protect you if questions come up later.

Step 2: Walk the property in a fixed order

Work through the property the same way every time, section by section, so nothing gets skipped. A common order is:

  1. Exterior: walls, cladding, windows, doors, gutters, drainage, driveways and paths.
  2. Roof: covering, flashing, vents and chimney, viewed from the ground or the roof where it is safe.
  3. Structure: foundations, visible framing, floors, and signs of movement or water.
  4. Plumbing: water pressure, drains, taps, visible pipes and the water heater.
  5. Electrical: panel, outlets, switches and visible wiring, with safety concerns flagged.
  6. Heating and cooling: systems, filters and operation.
  7. Interior: ceilings, walls, floors, doors, windows, kitchen, bathrooms and any signs of damp or mould.
  8. Attic, basement and crawl space: insulation, ventilation and moisture.

Step 3: Document each finding

Every issue should have enough evidence for the client to understand it without you there. For each finding, record:

  • Photos of the problem and its location, plus a wider shot for context.
  • A short video when the issue is intermittent, such as a noise or a leak that appears under load.
  • Notes that describe what you saw, where it is and what it might mean. Voice notes are faster when your hands are busy.

Step 4: Rate severity

Clients need to know what to fix first. Tag each finding by severity:

  • Minor: cosmetic or routine maintenance.
  • Major: a defect that needs a professional to repair or assess.
  • Safety: a hazard that needs attention before anyone uses that area.
Keep it factual. Describe what you observed and what you could not check. Avoid guessing about causes or costs unless you are qualified to do so.

Step 5: Write the summary

Finish with a short summary at the top of the report: the most important findings, the safety items, and any areas you could not access. A reader should understand the main points in under two minutes.

Step 6: Generate and send the PDF

Export the report as a PDF and send it to the client before you leave the site if you can. Include your branding and contact details, and keep a copy for your records.

Free home inspection checklist

Use this as a starting point. Adjust it to your local standards and the type of property.

  • Address, date, client and attendees recorded
  • Exterior walls, cladding, windows and doors checked
  • Gutters, drainage and grading checked
  • Roof covering, flashing and vents checked
  • Foundation and visible structure checked
  • Water pressure, drains and water heater checked
  • Electrical panel, outlets and switches tested
  • Heating and cooling operated
  • Interior rooms, kitchen and bathrooms checked
  • Attic, basement or crawl space checked for moisture
  • Each finding photographed and rated Minor, Major or Safety
  • Summary written and PDF sent to the client

Frequently asked questions

How long should a home inspection report be?

Long enough to cover every area you inspected and every finding, and short enough that the client can read the summary quickly. A summary at the top does most of the work.
